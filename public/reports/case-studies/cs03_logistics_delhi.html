02 The Problem
Challenge 1: Misclassification — Bank Tagged Them as "Trading Company"

The company's NIC code in their GST registration and their bank account description used the word "trading" — a legacy tag from when they started as a goods trader before pivoting to logistics. The private bank's credit officer classified them under the "trading" sector, applied more conservative lending norms (lower CC eligibility ratio, stricter collateral requirements), and declined the CC application citing insufficient stock/debtor security. In reality, this was a pure services business with no trading activity for 4 years.

Challenge 2: Debtors Were Not Being Pledged Correctly

The CC was sought against book debts — outstanding invoices from e-commerce and FMCG clients. However, the debtor statements submitted were not in the bank's required format, mixing domestic and credit card receivables. More critically, two of the largest debtors (₹8.4 Crore combined) were large-cap listed companies with 60-day payment terms — which should attract significantly lower credit risk weight but were being treated as high-risk debtors.

Challenge 3: Fleet as Primary Security — Bank Had No Valuation Framework

For the ₹10 Crore term loan for 45 additional vehicles, the bank lacked an internal framework for valuing a fleet of mixed commercial vehicles. The empanelled valuer had only valued real estate before. The vehicle portfolio was being collectively valued at depreciated book value (₹3.8 Crore), creating a massive apparent collateral shortfall on the TL.

03 What FinsightOne Did
Action 1: Reclassified the Business — Fixed the NIC Code Issue

We obtained a formal declaration from the company's CA confirming zero trading activity since FY21 and 100% revenue from logistics services. We assisted in filing a GST amendment to correct the primary NIC code to 4923 (Freight Transport by Road) — the correct classification. We then presented the application under the correct sector norms. RBI's guidelines under Priority Sector Lending classify logistics and transport companies separately from traders — the CC eligibility ratios are completely different and much more favourable.

Action 2: Rebuilt the Debtor Statement in Bank Format

We prepared the debtor aging statement in the exact format prescribed by the bank's credit manual, separating: (a) Large corporate debtors — listed companies, government bodies (₹14.8 Crore) and (b) SME/individual debtors (₹4.2 Crore). For large corporate debtors, we attached CIBIL TransUnion Corporate reports showing AAA/AA credit ratings, arguing for a lower advance rate (banks typically advance 75–80% against corporate debtors vs 50–60% against SME debtors). This increased the calculated CC eligibility from ₹9.6 Crore to ₹12.4 Crore.

Action 3: Arranged Commercial Vehicle-Specialist Valuer

We identified an RBI-empanelled valuer specialising in commercial vehicle fleets (they work with fleet finance companies) and arranged a proper fleet valuation. Market value of the 84-vehicle fleet was assessed at ₹18.2 Crore on going-concern basis. This, combined with the 3 owned warehouse properties (₹9.4 Crore), provided total collateral coverage of 127% for the combined ₹22 Crore facility.

The Analogy That Helped: The bank was treating the debtor statement like a single pile of mixed coins — valuing them all at the lowest common denomination. We separated the gold coins (listed corporates — Reliance, Flipkart Commerce) from the small change. When the credit officer saw that ₹14.8 Crore of the debtors were backed by companies with AAA ratings, the credit risk picture changed completely.
