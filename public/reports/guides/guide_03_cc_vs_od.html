💰 Working Capital

CC Limit vs OD Limit:
Which Is Right for Your Business?

The difference between a Cash Credit and an Overdraft facility — and when to choose one over the other. Most business owners get this wrong, and it costs them in interest and eligibility.

📖 6 min read
👤 For: Business owners needing working capital
💡 Includes: Real examples with numbers
The Simple Analogy First

Think of a Cash Credit (CC) like a warehouse receipt loan — you borrow against your stock and debtors. The bank knows what's securing the loan at all times and adjusts how much you can borrow based on inventory levels. It's linked to your business cycle.

An Overdraft (OD) is like a clean bridge loan — you borrow against property or a fixed asset, and the bank gives you a fixed limit. It's simpler to use but typically secured against property rather than business assets.

The key difference in one sentence: CC limit fluctuates based on your drawing power (stock + debtors); OD limit is fixed based on the value of the security you pledge.
Side-by-Side Comparison

How Drawing Power Works (CC Only)

This is the most misunderstood aspect of Cash Credit. Your drawing power — how much you can actually draw at any time — is not your limit. It changes every month based on what you report.

📋 Example: A Garment Trader with ₹1 Crore CC Limit

September (before Diwali season):
Stock value: ₹80 lakh | Debtors (under 90 days): ₹60 lakh
Drawing Power = (80% of stock) + (75% of debtors) = ₹64L + ₹45L = ₹1.09 Crore → Can draw up to ₹1 Crore (full limit)


March (post-season):
Stock value: ₹30 lakh | Debtors: ₹40 lakh
Drawing Power = (80% of 30L) + (75% of 40L) = ₹24L + ₹30L = ₹54 lakh → Can only draw ₹54 lakh even though limit is ₹1 Crore


If you've drawn ₹80 lakh in March and your DP drops to ₹54 lakh — the bank will ask for the excess ₹26 lakh to be brought back. This is why regular drawing power management matters.

⚠️ The Monthly Statement Trap: Many borrowers forget to submit the monthly stock/debtors statement. Banks then freeze the CC at the last DP or drop it to zero. This creates a cash flow crisis — not because your business is bad, but because you missed filing a form. Set a reminder: submit by the 7th of every month.
When to Choose CC vs OD

Choose Cash Credit (CC) if:

  • You're in manufacturing, trading, or exports
  • You hold inventory (raw material, finished goods)
  • You sell on credit and have outstanding debtors
  • Your working capital need fluctuates with the season
  • You want a larger limit (CC eligibility is linked to business size)

Choose Overdraft (OD) if:

  • You own property and want a simple credit line
  • You have Fixed Deposits you can pledge (FD OD is cheapest credit available)
  • You're a professional (doctor, CA, consultant) — no stock to hypothecate
  • You want a fixed, predictable limit with no monthly filing
  • You need bridging finance for a short period
The Cheapest Working Capital Available: If you have a Fixed Deposit of ₹50 lakh, you can get an OD against it at roughly 1% above the FD rate — typically 6.5–7.5% per annum. This is the lowest interest rate available for any working capital facility and most businesses with idle FDs should use this before applying for CC.
How CC Limit is Calculated — The Tandon Committee Method

RBI's credit norms for working capital are based on the Tandon Committee recommendations. Most PSU banks and many private banks use this for CC limit assessment.

Tandon Method II — Maximum Permissible Bank Finance (MPBF)

Step 1: Calculate Total Current Assets (TCA) = Inventory + Debtors + Other CA

Step 2: Calculate Total Current Liabilities (TCL) = Creditors + Other CL (excluding bank borrowings)

Step 3: Working Capital Gap = TCA − TCL

Step 4: Minimum Margin = 25% of TCA (your own contribution)

Step 5: MPBF = Working Capital Gap − Minimum Margin


Example: TCA ₹100L | TCL ₹20L | WC Gap ₹80L | Margin (25% of ₹100L) ₹25L | MPBF = ₹80L − ₹25L = ₹55L CC limit

RBI Reference: The Tandon Committee Report (1974) and subsequent RBI circulars form the basis of working capital assessment for bank credit. RBI's Master Circular on Loans and Advances (updated annually) provides the framework banks use for assessing MPBF for manufacturing and trading units.
