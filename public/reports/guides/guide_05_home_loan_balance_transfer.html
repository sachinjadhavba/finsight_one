Home Loan Balance Transfer:
When It Makes Sense (With Numbers)

How to calculate if a balance transfer actually saves you money — with a real example across a 15-year tenure. The math most people get wrong — and the hidden costs banks don't highlight.

📖 7 min read
👤 For: Anyone with a home loan paying above 8.5% interest
🔢 Includes: Full worked example with actual numbers
When Does a Balance Transfer Actually Make Sense?

A home loan balance transfer (BT) means moving your existing home loan from your current bank to a new lender offering a lower interest rate. In theory, lower rate = lower EMI = more savings. In practice, it's more nuanced — because there are transfer costs, and because of how home loan interest works, the timing of the transfer matters enormously.

The Core Rule: A balance transfer makes sense only if the interest savings over the remaining loan tenure exceed the total cost of transfer. And the earlier in your loan tenure you transfer, the more you save — because home loans front-load interest (you pay more interest in the early years).
The Full Worked Example — ₹50 Lakh Home Loan

Scenario: Should Ramesh Transfer His Home Loan?

Current situation: Ramesh took a ₹50 lakh home loan 3 years ago at 9.5% p.a. for a 20-year tenure. His EMI is ₹46,561/month. He has now received an offer from a new bank at 8.4% p.a.

Outstanding balance after 3 years: Approximately ₹47.8 lakh (principal reduces very slowly in the early years).

Remaining tenure: 17 years

Now subtract the costs of transfer:

Processing fee at new bank (0.5% of ₹47.8L)₹23,900

The Final Calculation

Total interest saving over 17 years: ₹8.05 Lakh

Transfer cost: ~ ₹50,000

Net saving: ₹7.55 Lakh ✓ — Transfer makes strong financial sense.

The transfer cost is recovered in just 14 months (₹50,000 ÷ ₹3,720/month savings). After that, every rupee of savings is net benefit.

The Hidden Costs Banks Don't Emphasise
  • Prepayment penalty on fixed rate loans: If your current home loan is on a fixed rate (not floating), the bank can charge a prepayment penalty of up to 2% of outstanding principal. On ₹47.8 lakh, that's ₹95,600 — which significantly erodes the savings. RBI has banned prepayment penalties only for floating-rate loans.
  • MODT (Memorandum of Deposit of Title Deeds) stamp duty: When you transfer, the new bank registers a fresh mortgage on your property. In states like Maharashtra, stamp duty on MODT can be 0.1–0.3% of loan amount — check your state's rate before calculating savings.
  • Insurance reset: Your current bank may have bundled a home loan insurance (HLPP). Transferring means this policy may lapse. Factor in the cost of a new policy or check if the existing policy is transferable.
  • Reset of repayment history: If you're close to completing your loan or have a short remaining tenure (under 5 years), the math rarely works in favour of a BT — the transfer costs eat up most of the interest savings on the small remaining principal.
⚠️ When NOT to Transfer: Remaining tenure under 5 years (too little principal left to justify transfer costs). Current rate already below 8.5%. Fixed rate loan with prepayment penalty. Property documents have any title dispute (new bank's legal team will flag it). Outstanding balance under ₹20 lakh (costs are proportionally too high).
5 Things to Negotiate with the New Bank
  • Processing fee waiver or reduction — most banks will negotiate, especially if your CIBIL is 750+. Processing fees are not fixed — they're a starting point for negotiation.
  • Interest rate with no reset clause — some banks offer a lower rate but include a clause allowing them to reset after 3 years. Insist on MCLR/repo-linked rate with transparent reset mechanism.
  • Top-up loan at the same rate — if you need additional funds, negotiate a top-up at the same concessional rate at the time of transfer. Getting a top-up later is harder.
  • Legal and technical charges waived — for loans above ₹30 lakh, banks often waive these as part of the offer.
  • Get the rate in writing — verbal offers don't matter. The rate in the sanction letter is the rate you actually get. Never start the transfer process without a written sanction letter from the new bank.
